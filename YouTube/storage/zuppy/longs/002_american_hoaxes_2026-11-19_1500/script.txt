In eighteen thirty-five, a respected New York newspaper told its readers that bat-winged people lived on the Moon. It described blue unicorns, beavers that walked on two legs, and a glittering temple with a golden roof. Thousands of people believed every word, and they paid a penny a day to read more. Over the next hundred years, Americans would fall for talking ghosts, a giant buried in a farm field, a miracle cure made from snakes, and an alien invasion on the radio. Stay until the end, because the last story is a hoax about a hoax.

Before we meet the liars, we need to understand the crowd. In the eighteen hundreds, there was no television, no internet, and no way to check a story in seconds. If a trusted newspaper printed something, most people assumed it was true. Wonder was also big business, because new telescopes, new machines, and new discoveries seemed to appear every year. When the real world keeps surprising you, a fake surprise slips right in.

The first hoax came from a new kind of newspaper called the penny press. In eighteen thirty-three, a printer named Benjamin Day launched The New York Sun and sold it for just one cent, far cheaper than older papers. Cheap papers needed huge crowds to earn money from advertisements, so editors hunted for shocking stories. The Sun already printed crime, scandal, and strange news that fancier papers avoided. All it needed was one story big enough to make the whole city talk.

On August twenty-fifth, eighteen thirty-five, the Sun began a series of six articles about a stunning discovery. The articles claimed that Sir John Herschel, a real and famous British astronomer, had pointed a gigantic telescope at the Moon and seen life. The details came from a Doctor Andrew Grant, who was invented by the writer, and from a science journal that had actually stopped publishing years before. Herschel really was working in South Africa at the time, so the lie wore a costume made of truth. Most historians say the author was a Sun reporter named Richard Adams Locke, who did not sign his name.

Each day, the story grew wilder. Readers met blue goat-like unicorns, and beavers that walked upright and lived in huts with chimneys. Then came the stars of the show, winged people called man-bats, who seemed to talk, wave their arms, and gather by a lake. Locke wrote in a calm, scientific style, with measurements and long technical words, so it felt more like a lab report than a fairy tale. People crowded around newsstands, and sales of the Sun jumped.

So, did everyone really believe it? Not everyone, but many did, and other papers copied the story as if it were real news. Some visitors, including scholars, traveled to the Sun's office asking to see the original journal, and they were told it was still at the printer. Religious groups reportedly even talked about sending missionaries to the Moon. On September sixteenth, the Sun admitted it was a hoax, and most readers simply laughed.

The real Herschel was said to be amused at first, then annoyed, as letters from believers kept arriving. Locke later said he meant it as a joke on far-fetched astronomy claims of the time, but he had badly underestimated how much people wanted it to be true. Remember that lesson, because it comes back in every story. A trusted voice, a bit of real science, and a wonderful fantasy make a very powerful mix.

Now let's jump ahead thirteen years, to a small farmhouse in Hydesville, New York. In March of eighteen forty-eight, two sisters named Maggie and Kate Fox, aged fourteen and eleven, told their mother that a ghost was knocking in the house. Their mother asked the ghost questions, and it answered with knocks. Neighbors poured in to listen, and soon the knocks were said to come from a murdered peddler. A farmhouse mystery had just become national news.

Their older sister Leah took charge of the show, and moved the girls to Rochester and then to New York City. Soon the sisters were holding seances, which are gatherings where a medium claims to speak with the dead, for paying guests. The craze was called Spiritualism, and within a few years it had followers across the country. Many families were grieving, because disease took so many people, and a message from a lost loved one felt like medicine for the heart. Spiritualism grew into a movement with newspapers, meetings, and followers by the thousands.

Forty years later, Maggie shocked the nation. On October twenty-first, eighteen eighty-eight, she stood on the stage of the New York Academy of Music in front of about two thousand people. In her stocking feet, she cracked the joints of her toes, and the loud raps echoed through the hall. Doctors from the audience came up to check, and they agreed the sounds came from her toes. She said the whole thing began as a prank on their mother.

You might expect Spiritualism to collapse that night, but instead it kept growing. Maggie was reportedly paid for her confession, she was struggling with poverty and alcohol, and a year later she took it back. Believers argued that the sisters had been genuine at first and had only faked it later. The sisters died poor and largely forgotten, yet the movement they started outlived them. Here is the lesson: when a belief gives comfort, a confession alone is not enough to kill it.

Our third hoax began with an argument about the Bible. In eighteen sixty-eight, a cigar maker named George Hull, an atheist from Binghamton, New York, argued with a believer who insisted that giants once walked the Earth. Hull decided to give believers their giant. He had a huge block of gypsum carved into a ten-foot man, then roughened and stained it so it looked ancient. The whole project cost him about twenty-six hundred dollars, a small fortune at the time.

He shipped the giant by train to his cousin's farm in Cardiff, New York, and buried it in the ground. A year later, in October of eighteen sixty-nine, workers hired to dig a well uncovered a giant stone man. Within days, the farm was charging visitors admission, and the crowds were enormous. Preachers said it proved the Bible's giants were real, while some scientists called it a fake right away. But people kept buying tickets, because it was a great show.

A group of investors led by David Hannum bought the giant and moved it to Syracuse, where ticket sales kept rising. The famous showman Barnum offered a huge sum for it, reportedly fifty thousand dollars, and Hannum said no. So Barnum did something wonderfully shameless, and made a plaster copy, displayed it in New York, and called his the real one. Hannum sued, and was quoted making fun of the crowds who paid to see Barnum's copy, saying that a sucker is born every minute. People still blame Barnum for that famous line, but the words seem to belong to Hannum.

The truth came out on December tenth, eighteen sixty-nine, when George Hull confessed to the press. In February, a judge ruled that Barnum could not be sued for calling a fake giant a fake. In the end, everyone had been fooled, including the man who owned the original fake. Today the original giant sits in the Farmers' Museum in Cooperstown, New York, and visitors still line up to see it.

Not every hoax needed a giant, because some only needed a bottle. In eighteen ninety-three, a showman named Clark Stanley, who called himself the Rattlesnake King, became a star at the World's Fair in Chicago. In front of a crowd, he pulled a live rattlesnake from a sack, sliced it open, and dropped it into boiling water so the fat floated to the top. He skimmed the fat, mixed it into a liniment, and sold bottles of Stanley's Snake Oil to the people watching. Historians say the snake act was only a show, and the liniment he sold had been prepared ahead of time.

There was a twist, because real snake oil did exist. Chinese railroad workers in America used oil from the Chinese water snake, which is rich in omega-three fats, to ease their sore joints. American salesmen copied the idea with rattlesnakes, whose oil has much less of those healthy fats. In nineteen seventeen, years after the Pure Food and Drug Act, federal investigators seized a shipment of Stanley's bottles and tested it. They found mostly mineral oil, beef fat, red pepper, and turpentine, but no snake oil. Stanley was fined twenty dollars, and the words snake oil became a nickname for any fake cure.

Our final story happened on October thirtieth, nineteen thirty-eight, the night before Halloween. A twenty-three-year-old director named Orson Welles put on a radio drama based on the novel The War of the Worlds. It was staged like a real news broadcast, with reporters describing Martians landing in New Jersey. The next morning, newspapers across America reported that thousands of people had panicked, fled their homes, and flooded police stations. For decades, textbooks and documentaries repeated that Welles had terrified a nation. But historians who studied the evidence found a very different story.

That night, a ratings company called Hooper telephoned five thousand households and asked what people were listening to. Only about two percent said they were tuning in to Welles, while many were hearing a popular comedy show on another station. Some listeners really were frightened, and some called police and newspapers, but scholars say a check of six New York hospitals found no cases linked to the broadcast. Many media historians argue that newspapers inflated the panic, because they were fighting radio for advertising money and wanted to show that radio could not be trusted. A famous book by the psychologist Hadley Cantril in nineteen forty helped turn the legend into accepted fact, even though critics say his numbers were weak. So the biggest hoax of all may be the story we tell about the people who fell for a hoax.

So why do smart people fall for these stories? First, we trust sources that look official, whether that is a newspaper, a scientist's name, or a confident voice on the radio. Second, we love a story that matches what we already hope is true, like life on the Moon, messages from lost loved ones, or proof of ancient giants. Third, once we have shared a story, admitting we were wrong feels embarrassing, so many people simply keep believing. And finally, a hoax that is fun, mysterious, or comforting travels faster than a boring correction.

The good news is that you can protect yourself with a few simple questions. Who is telling this story, and how do they actually know? Is there a second, independent source, or is everyone copying the same claim? Who gains money or attention if I believe this, and does it seem too perfect? Pause for ten seconds before sharing, because that tiny delay stops many hoaxes.

From moon men to toe-cracking ghosts, from stone giants to snake oil, American history is full of tall tales that people loved. The hoaxers were clever, but the real engine was always us, with our curiosity, our hope, and our wish for wonder. Curiosity is never the problem, as long as it comes with a little healthy doubt.
